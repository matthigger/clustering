Clustering, visually

No labels, just samples: group them so each sample sits close to its cluster's mean. Watch k-means and Ward's method do it one step at a time.

k-means

Goal: split the samples into k clusters C1, ..., Ck with each sample close to its cluster's mean μj (its centroid): the smallest within-cluster sum of squares.

Approach: (Lloyd) start from k means, then alternate two steps until no sample switches cluster. Neither step can raise WCSS, so it always stops, though not always at the best clustering.

WCSS = ∑j ∑xi ∈ Cj ‖xi − μj‖2
assign: xi joins the Cj with the smallest ‖xi − μj‖2 ⇒ update: μj = 1Nj ∑xi ∈ Cj xi

Nj is the number of samples in Cj. The shading shows which mean is nearest everywhere: the regions the next assign step uses.

Ward's method

Goal: a hierarchy of clusterings, from every sample alone (N clusters) down to one cluster, each made from the one before by merging two clusters.

Approach: (Ward 1963) start with each sample its own cluster, total WCSS 0. Repeat: merge the two clusters whose merge raises the total WCSS least.

ΔWCSS(A, B) = NA NBNA + NB ‖μA − μB‖2

The dendrogram joins two clusters with a bar at the total WCSS after their merge. Cut across at any height: the branches below the cut are the clusters. (scipy draws its bars at √(2 ΔWCSS) instead: same tree, different heights.)

Ward's method on a picture

Goal: describe the picture with N superpixels: patches painted one color each, as close as possible to the pixels' true colors.

Approach: each pixel is a sample, its color xi = (red, green, blue), each from 0 to 1. Ward's method merges the cheapest pair, but only of regions that touch, so every cluster is one connected patch.

merge the touching A, B with the smallest NA NBNA + NB ‖μA − μB‖2

One run gives every N at once: the slider just stops the merges early. Everything runs in your browser; an uploaded picture never leaves your computer.

Try this

  • On three blobs, press Play and watch the WCSS chart. Does WCSS ever go up?
    AnswerNever. Assign gives each sample its smallest squared distance among the means; update moves each mean to its cluster's average, the point with the smallest sum of squared distances to the cluster. Both steps can only lower the total, so the run must stop.
  • Now pick the start two in one blob and play. Where does it stop?
    AnswerWith one blob split in two and the other two blobs sharing one mean. No sample wants to switch, so k-means is stuck: a local minimum, with a higher WCSS than a good run (compare in Finished runs). The fix: run from several starts and keep the lowest WCSS, or start with k-means++.
  • Pick lesson 22 ICA and run both starts. Which is better?
    AnswerThe ICA start stops at WCSS 7.33, the extra start at 9.5. Both converged; only the start differs.
  • On how many?, run each k from 1 to 8 (press New start a few times for each). Where is the elbow in Finished runs?
    AnswerAround 4 or 5: two of the five blobs nearly touch, so 4 and 5 are both fair answers. WCSS always falls as k grows (at k = N it is 0), so look for where it stops falling fast.
  • Try stretched, moons and rings. Does k-means find the shapes you see?
    AnswerNo. The boundary between two clusters is the line halfway between their means (the shading), so every cluster is a convex region around its mean. On stretched, cutting across the bars gives a lower WCSS than the bars themselves: k-means is not wrong about WCSS, WCSS is the wrong goal for these shapes.
  • On one big, two small, press New start a few times with k-means++. Where do the big blob's edge samples go?
    AnswerIn the best runs (lowest WCSS), to the small clusters: a boundary sits halfway between two means no matter how spread out each cluster is, so the tight clusters claim the big one's edge. Other starts stop with the big blob cut in half and the two small ones sharing a mean. The three true groups have a higher WCSS than both.
  • Drag a mean to make your own start, then hover any log line to see that move on the plot.
  • On lesson 22 walk, step through. Where do the bars land?
    AnswerAt total WCSS 2, 6, 14 and 30, as in the lesson: {x₂, x₃}, then {x₄, x₅}, then those two, then x₁ last.
  • On three blobs (log axis), find the tallest gap between bars and drag the cut into it. How many clusters?
    AnswerThree. The next merge would multiply the total WCSS by more than 5: it joins two groups that really are apart. On the log axis, equal gaps are equal ratios.
  • Hover the merges late in the log. Is the cheapest pair always the pair with the closest means?
    AnswerNo. The factor NA NB / (NA + NB) grows with the clusters' sizes: two big clusters cost more to merge than two single samples the same distance apart, since merging moves the mean of every sample in both.
  • Switch the dendrogram to linear. Why is log the default?
    AnswerTotal WCSS grows quickly: the last few merges dwarf the rest, and on a linear axis the first dozens of merges are flat on the floor.
  • Try moons, rings and no clusters.
    AnswerLike k-means, Ward's looks for compact, round clusters, so it cuts the moons and rings across. On no clusters there is no tall gap: no number of clusters stands out.
  • Slide N down from every pixel. How few superpixels before the picture stops being recognizable? Press and hold the picture to compare with the original.
  • Turn on outlines at N = 50. Where are the superpixels small, and where big?
    AnswerSmall where the color changes fast (eyes, edges, text), big where it is smooth (background, fur). A merge in a smooth area costs little WCSS, so Ward's makes those first.
  • Why only merge regions that touch?
    AnswerWithout the rule, Ward's would group same-colored pixels from anywhere: N colors for the whole picture, not N patches. With it, two eyes of the same color stay two regions, and each region is a piece of the picture.
  • Look at the chart. Where does the total WCSS climb fastest?
    AnswerAt the last few merges, which join big regions of different colors. Merging thousands of near-identical neighbors early on is nearly free.
