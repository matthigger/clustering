Clustering, visually

No labels, just samples: group them so each sample sits close to its cluster's mean. Watch k-means and Ward's method do it one step at a time.

k-means

Goal: split the samples into k clusters C1, ..., Ck with each sample close to its cluster's mean μj (its centroid): the smallest within-cluster sum of squares.

Approach: (Lloyd) start from k means, then alternate two steps until no sample switches cluster. Neither step can raise WCSS, so it always stops, though not always at the best clustering.

WCSS = ∑j ∑xi ∈ Cj ‖xi − μj‖2
assign: xi joins the Cj with the smallest ‖xi − μj‖2 ⇒ update: μj = 1Nj ∑xi ∈ Cj xi

Nj is the number of samples in Cj. The shading shows which mean is nearest everywhere: the regions the next assign step uses.

Ward's method

Goal: a hierarchy of clusterings, from every sample alone (N clusters) down to one cluster, each made from the one before by merging two clusters.

Approach: (Ward 1963) start with each sample its own cluster, total WCSS 0. Repeat: merge the two clusters whose merge raises the total WCSS least.

ΔWCSS(A, B) = NA NBNA + NB ‖μA − μB‖2

The dendrogram joins two clusters with a bar at the total WCSS after their merge. Cut across at any height: the branches below the cut are the clusters. (scipy draws its bars at √(2 ΔWCSS) instead: same tree, different heights.)

Ward's method on a picture

Goal: describe the picture with N superpixels: patches painted one color each, as close as possible to the pixels' true colors.

Approach: each pixel is a sample, its color xi = (red, green, blue), each from 0 to 1. Ward's method merges the cheapest pair, but only of regions that touch, so every cluster is one connected patch.

merge the touching A, B with the smallest NA NBNA + NB ‖μA − μB‖2

One run gives every N at once: the slider just stops the merges early. Everything runs in your browser; an uploaded picture never leaves your computer.

Try this

  • On three blobs, press ▶ and watch WCSS by step. Does WCSS ever go up?
    AnswerNever. Assign gives each sample its nearest mean; update moves each mean to its cluster's average, the point with the smallest sum of squared distances to the cluster. So the run must stop.
  • Pick the start two in one blob. Where does it stop?
    AnswerWith one blob split in two and the other two sharing a mean: no sample wants to switch, yet the WCSS is far above a good run's (a local minimum). The fix: several starts, keeping the lowest WCSS, or k-means++.
  • On how many?, finish runs for each k from 1 to 8. Where is the elbow in Finished runs?
    AnswerAround 4 or 5: two of the blobs nearly touch. WCSS always falls as k grows, so look for where it stops falling fast.
  • Try stretched, moons and rings.
    Answerk-means cuts them across: each cluster is the region nearest its mean (the shading), a convex patch. On stretched the cut even has a lower WCSS than the three bars: WCSS is the wrong goal for these shapes.
  • On five points, step through. Why is each bar higher than the one before?
    AnswerEach merge adds its ΔWCSS, never negative, to the total, so the heights only go up.
  • On three blobs, find the tallest gap between bars and drag the cut into it. How many clusters?
    AnswerThree. The next merge would multiply the total WCSS by more than 5: it joins groups that really are apart. (On the log axis, equal gaps are equal ratios.)
  • Try moons and rings.
    AnswerLike k-means, Ward's looks for compact, round clusters, so it cuts these shapes across.
  • Slide N down. How few superpixels before the picture stops being recognizable? Press and hold the picture to compare with the original.
  • Turn on outlines at N = 50. Where are the superpixels small, and where big?
    AnswerSmall where the color changes fast (eyes, edges), big where it is smooth: a merge there costs little WCSS, so Ward's makes it first.
  • Why only merge regions that touch?
    AnswerOtherwise Ward's would group same-colored pixels from anywhere: N colors, not N patches.
